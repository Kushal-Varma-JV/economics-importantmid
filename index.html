Micro Ch 3 · Production and CostsUsually Q37 (6 marks)

Explain the various short run costs.6 marksAsked 4× · 2 board exams

Asked in: E26-2, E25-2, MQP24-1, MQP-4 (all Q37)
Also asked as: "Analyse the short run cost curves of TFC, TVC, SAC and SMC with the help of diagrams." (MQP-4)

Board answer key · E25-2

In short run only few inputs can be varied, keeping the others constant. The various short run costs are:

  1. Total Fixed cost (TFC)
  2. Total Variable cost (TVC)
  3. Total Cost (TC)
  4. Average Fixed Cost (AFC)
  5. Average Variable Cost (AVC)
  6. Short run Average Cost (SAC)
  7. Short run Marginal Cost (SMC)
Explanation · NCERT
  1. Total Fixed Cost (TFC): cost of the fixed inputs, such as rent of the building. It stays the same at every level of output, even at zero output. Its curve is a horizontal straight line.
  2. Total Variable Cost (TVC): cost of the variable inputs, such as labour and raw materials. It is zero at zero output and rises as output rises.
  3. Total Cost: TC = TFC + TVC. At zero output TC equals TFC. The TC curve has the same shape as TVC, lifted up by the amount of TFC.
  4. Average Fixed Cost: AFC = TFC ÷ q. TFC is constant, so AFC keeps falling as output rises. The AFC curve is a rectangular hyperbola (AFC × q always equals TFC).
  5. Average Variable Cost: AVC = TVC ÷ q. It falls first and then rises, so the curve is U-shaped.
  6. Short run Average Cost: SAC = TC ÷ q = AVC + AFC. U-shaped. It lies above AVC and the gap between them is AFC. The lowest point of SAC is to the right of the lowest point of AVC.
  7. Short run Marginal Cost: SMC = ΔTC ÷ Δq (TC at q minus TC at q−1). It is the extra cost of producing one more unit. SMC is U-shaped because of the law of variable proportions. It cuts AVC and SAC from below at their minimum points.

Explain the shapes of Total Product (TP), Average Product (AP) and Marginal Product (MP) curves.6 marksAsked 3× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E26-1, MQP-1, MQP-2 (all Q37)
Also asked as: "Explain the Law of Variable Proportions with the help of a diagram." (MQP-2)
Also covers: "State the law that explains the short-run production analysis." (1 mark) → Law of variable proportions

Board answer key · E26-1

According to the law of variable proportions, an increase in the amount of one of the inputs keeping all other inputs constant results in an increase in total output. As shown in the diagram the total product TP curve is a positively sloped curve.

  • Marginal product MP of an input initially rises and then after a certain level of employment of input, it starts falling.
  • AP being the average of MP, also rises, But since it increases at a lesser rate than MP, the AP curve lies below the MP curve. After a certain point, when MP starts to decrease, AP also starts decrease at lesser rate than MP.
  • MP curve cuts AP curve from above at its maximum. In this way MP and AP curves are in inverse 'U'-shape.
Explanation · NCERT

Meanings: TP is the output from different amounts of the variable input, other inputs fixed. APL = TPL ÷ L. MPL = ΔTPL ÷ ΔL = TP at L − TP at L−1.

Law of variable proportions (law of diminishing marginal product): when one input is increased and the others are kept fixed, the marginal product of that input first rises, and after a certain level of employment it starts falling.

Why it happens: the ratio of the inputs (factor proportions) changes. Take a farmer with 4 hectares of land. With one worker there is too much land per worker, so each extra worker adds more and more output. After a point the land gets crowded and each extra worker adds less.

In a perfectly competitive market, when market price of each unit of good is Rs. 30, compute the Total Revenue (TR), Marginal Revenue (MR) and Average Revenue (AR) schedules.Price changes between papers: Rs 30, 25, 50 (QB: 60). Quantity sold = 0 to 5.5 marksAsked 3× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-2 (Q41), MQP24-1 (Q41), MQP24-2 (Q42). Not used in the 2026-27 model papers.

Method: TR = Price × q. MR = TR − previous TR. AR = TR ÷ q. A perfectly competitive firm sells every unit at the same market price, so MR = AR = Price.

Part F version (visually challenged students): "Give the meaning of TR, MR and AR." Asked in E25-2, MQP24-1, MQP24-2.

Board answer key · E25-2
  1. Total Revenue: is defined as the revenue earned by selling all units of output produced by a firm. TR = P x Q
  2. Marginal Revenue: is defined as the additional revenue earned by the sale of additional unit. MR = ∆TR/∆q or MR = TRn – TRn-1
  3. Average Revenue: is defined as the revenue earned per unit of output sold. AR = TR/q

⚠️ The English key writes "MPL = TRn – TRn-1". It should say MR.

Draw a diagram for the following table and identify the equilibrium point, equilibrium price, equilibrium quantity, excess demand and excess supply in the diagram.A new P–QD–QS table every paper (5 prices).5 marksAsked 7× · 2 board exams

Asked in: E26-1, E26-2, MQP-1, MQP-2, MQP-3, MQP-4, MQP-5 (all Q42)

How to answer:

  1. Price on the Y-axis, quantity on the X-axis.
  2. Plot the QD points and join them: the demand curve DD slopes down.
  3. Plot the QS points and join them: the supply curve SS slopes up.
  4. Where DD and SS cross is the equilibrium point E. Read off the equilibrium price and quantity.
  5. Above E, QS > QD, so mark excess supply. Below E, QD > QS, so mark excess demand.

Part F version (visually challenged students): "Give the meaning of market equilibrium, equilibrium price, equilibrium quantity, excess demand and excess supply." Asked in all 7 papers.

Board answer key · E26-1
  1. Market Equilibrium is a situation where the plans of all consumers and firms in the markets match. Or the situation where the market demand equals market supply.
  2. Equilibrium Price is the price at which the market demand equals market supply.
  3. Equilibrium quantity is the quantity bought and sold at equilibrium price.
  4. Excess Demand is the situation where market demand exceeds market supply.
  5. Excess Supply is the situation where market supply exceeds market demand.
Suppose the demand and supply curves of salt are given by qD = 100 − P and qS = 70 + 2Pa) Find the equilibrium price and quantity
b) Suppose the government has imposed a tax of Rs.3 per unit of sale of salt, how does it affect the equilibrium price and quantity?
MQP24-2 asked a wheat version: QD = 250 − P, QS = 150 + P6 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-2 (Q38), MQP24-2 (Q38, wheat)

Board answer key · E25-2 (salt)

a) qD = qS → 100 − P = 70 + 2P → 30 = 3P → P = 10.
qD = 100 − 10 = 90 and qS = 70 + 2(10) = 90. The equilibrium quantity is 90.

b) With a tax of Rs 3, sellers keep only P − 3 per unit, so the supply becomes qS = 70 + 2(P − 3).
100 − P = 70 + 2P − 6 → 100 − P = 64 + 2P → 36 = 3P → P = 12.
qD = 100 − 12 = 88 and qS = 70 + 2(9) = 88.
Therefore, when tax of Rs. 3 is imposed, equilibrium price is 12 and new equilibrium quantity is 88. The price rises and the quantity falls.

Worked out · MQP24-2 wheat version

a. Find the equilibrium price.
QD = QS → 250 − P = 150 + P → 100 = 2P → P = 50

b. Find the equilibrium quantity of demand and supply.
QD = 250 − 50 = 200, QS = 150 + 50 = 200 → 200

c. Find the quantity of demand and supply when P is greater than equilibrium price.
Take P = 60: QD = 190, QS = 210 → QS > QD, an excess supply of 20.

d. Find the quantity of demand and supply when P is lesser than equilibrium price.
Take P = 40: QD = 210, QS = 190 → QD > QS, an excess demand of 20.

Explain the law of Diminishing Marginal Utility with the help of table and diagram.6 marksAsked 4× · 3 of the 4 board exams

Asked in: E26-1, E26-2, E25-2, MQP24-3 (all Q36)
Also asked as: "Present the law of diminishing marginal utility with the help of a table and diagram."

Board answer key · E26-1

Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility states that marginal utility from consuming each additional unit of a commodity declines as its consumption increases, while keeping consumption of other commodities constant. Usually, the marginal utility diminishes with increase in consumption of the commodity. This happens because having obtained some amount of the commodity, the desire of the consumer to have still more of it becomes weaker. The same is shown in the table and graph.

Explanation · NCERT
  • Total utility (TU): the total satisfaction from consuming a given amount of a commodity.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from consuming one more unit. MUn = TUn − TUn−1. Also, TUn = MU1 + MU2 + … + MUn.
  • Reading the table: MU falls from 12 to 6 to 4 to 2, so TU rises, but at a diminishing rate.
  • At the 5th unit TU stays at 24, so MU = 0 and TU is at its maximum.
  • At the 6th unit TU falls to 22, so MU becomes negative (−2).
  • Why MU falls: once the consumer has some of the good, her desire for still more of it becomes weaker.
  • Use of the law: it explains the downward-sloping demand curve. Each extra unit gives less utility, so the consumer buys more only at a lower price.
Explain the optimal choice of consumer with the help of diagram.6 marksAsked 3× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-3, MQP24-1, MQP-4 (all Q36)
Also covers: "Explain the derivation of demand curve from Indifference curves and budget constraints." (MQP-3). See the note at the end.

Board answer key · E25-3

The budget line shows the combinations available to the consumer at the same level of income. The indifference curve represents the consumer's preference over the available combinations. When a consumer is aware of the indifference curve and the budget line, the consumer chooses above the high indifference curve and within the budget line. This represents the consumer's unitary preference. The consumer's ideal choice is the point on the high indifference curve that touches the budget line.

Explanation · NCERT
  • The consumer is rational: from the bundles she can afford, she chooses the one that gives maximum satisfaction. Her problem is to reach the highest possible indifference curve given her budget set.
  • The optimum is on the budget line. A point below it is not best: with monotonic preferences there is always a point on the line with more of at least one good. Points above the line cannot be afforded.
  • The optimum is where the budget line is tangent to an indifference curve (point E on IC2). Any other point on the budget line lies on a lower curve, such as IC1. IC3 is out of reach.
  • Condition at E: slope of IC = slope of budget line, i.e. MRS = p1/p2. MRS is the rate at which the consumer is willing to substitute. p1/p2 is the rate at which the market allows her to substitute.
  • Why they must be equal: suppose MRS = 2 and both goods cost the same. The consumer would give up 2 mangoes for 1 extra banana, but the market asks for only 1 mango. By buying the banana she gets more of both goods than before. So a point where MRS ≠ price ratio cannot be the optimum.

Deriving the demand curve (MQP-3): keep income and the price of X2 fixed and lower the price of X1. The budget line swings outward, and the new tangency is on a higher IC with more X1. Plotting each price of X1 against the quantity of X1 chosen gives a downward-sloping demand curve. The substitution effect and the income effect both raise demand when the price falls.

Illustrate the movement along the demand curve and shift in demand curve with the help of two diagrams.6 marksAsked 2× · model papers only

Asked in: MQP24-2 (Q36), MQP-2 (Q36)

Answer · NCERT

The quantity demanded depends on the good's own price, the prices of other goods, the consumer's income and her tastes and preferences. The demand curve is drawn keeping everything except the good's own price constant.

1. Movement along the demand curve: happens when only the price of the good changes. The consumer moves to another point on the same curve.

  • Price falls → quantity demanded rises (downward movement, extension of demand).
  • Price rises → quantity demanded falls (upward movement, contraction of demand).

2. Shift in the demand curve: happens when a factor other than the good's own price changes. The whole curve moves.

  • Rightward shift (more demanded at every price): income rises for a normal good, the price of a substitute rises, the price of a complement falls, or tastes change in favour of the good (e.g. ice-cream in summer).
  • Leftward shift (less demanded at every price): income rises for an inferior good, the price of a complement rises, or tastes turn against the good (e.g. news that cold drinks harm health).
