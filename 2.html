Micro Ch 4 · Theory of the Firm under Perfect CompetitionUsually Q29

Explain the determinants of a firm's supply curve.4 marksAsked 3× · 2 board exams · same topic in 6 more short questions

Asked in: E26-1, E25-3, MQP-1 (all Q29)
Also covers: "Mention the two determinants…" (2 marks) and "To which side does a supply curve shift due to technological progress?" (1 mark)

Board answer key · E26-1

Since the supply curve of a firm is a part of the MC curve, the factor affecting the MC curve becomes the determinant of the supply curve. Two such factors are discussed here.

a) Technological progress: A firm produces more units with the same level of capital and labour through new technology. This reduces the marginal cost of the firm. Technological progress shifts the supply curve of the firm to the right.

b) Input prices: If the price of an input (e.g., the wage rate of labour) increases, the cost of production also increases. This causes the average cost at any level of output of the firm to increase and the marginal cost to increase. This means that the firm's supply curve shifts to the left.

Explanation · NCERT
  • A firm's supply curve is the rising part of its MC curve above minimum AVC. So anything that shifts the MC curve shifts the supply curve.
  • Technological progress: an innovation lets the same capital and labour produce more, so fewer inputs are needed for a given output. MC falls and shifts right (down), so the supply curve shifts right: more is supplied at every price.
  • Input prices: a rise in, say, the wage rate raises the cost of production. AC and MC rise and MC shifts left (up), so the supply curve shifts left: less is supplied at every price. A fall in input prices does the opposite.
  • Extra point, unit tax: a tax of Rs t per unit raises AC and MC by t at every output, so the supply curve shifts left.
Write about Shut down point, Normal profit and Break-even point.4 marksAsked 2× · model papers only

Asked in: MQP-3 (Q29), MQP24-2 (Q29)
Also covers: "Give the meaning of shut down point" (2 marks, asked 3×) and the "Normal profit" match item

Shut down point · Board answer key E26-1

In the short run, the minimum point of the AVC is the last price and output combination, where the SMC curve cuts the AVC curve at this minimum point. In the long run, the LRMC curve cuts the LRAC curve at its minimum point. This is called the shut down point because there is no production below this point.

Normal profit and break-even point · NCERT

Normal profit: the minimum level of profit needed to keep a firm in its existing business. A firm that does not earn it will not stay in business, so normal profit is counted as part of the firm's total cost. It can be thought of as the opportunity cost of entrepreneurship. Profit above normal profit is called super-normal profit. In the long run a firm will not produce if it earns less than normal profit. In the short run it may continue.

Break-even point: the point on the supply curve at which the firm earns only normal profit (zero super-normal profit). It is the point of minimum average cost, where the supply (MC) curve cuts the LRAC curve (the SAC curve in the short run). Here price = AC, so TR = TC.

What is price ceiling? Explain with a diagram.4 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-3 (Q30), MQP-2 (Q30)

Board answer key · E25-3

A price ceiling is an upper limit imposed by the government on the price of a good or service. Generally, a price ceiling is imposed on essential commodities like wheat, rice, kerosene, sugar, etc. The price is fixed at a level lower than the market price. Example: Public Distribution System.

Explanation · NCERT
  • The market equilibrium is at E: price P*, quantity q*. At P* some poor people cannot afford necessities, so the government fixes a maximum price Pc below P*.
  • At Pc consumers demand qc but firms supply only q'c, so there is an excess demand (shortage) of q'c qc.
  • The limited supply is shared out by rationing: ration coupons are issued and the good is sold through ration shops (fair price shops).
  • Bad effects: (a) consumers stand in long queues at ration shops; (b) people who do not get enough are willing to pay more, which can create a black market.
What is price floor? Explain with a diagram.4 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E26-2 (Q30), MQP24-1 (Q30)
Also asked as: "Write about the concept of Price Floor." (MQP24-1)

Board answer key · E26-2

The lower limit imposed on the price of a good by the government is called Price floor. It is marked above the equilibrium level. EX: MSP and Minimum Wages Act.

Explanation · NCERT
  • For some goods, a very low price is not desirable, so the government sets a minimum price above the equilibrium price p*.
  • At pf consumers demand only qf but firms want to supply q'f, so there is an excess supply of qf q'f.
  • Examples: agricultural price support (Minimum Support Price for farmers), and minimum wage legislation (the wage is kept above the equilibrium wage).
  • Under price support, the government must buy the surplus at the fixed price to stop the price from falling.

Present the derivation of the slope of the Budget line.4 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-2 (Q29), MQP24-1 (Q28)

Board answer key · E25-2

The slope of the budget line measures the amount of change in mangoes required per unit of change in bananas along the budget line. Consider any two points (x1, x2) and (x1 + Δx1, x2 + Δx2) on the budget line.

p1x1 + p2x2 = M ……(2.4)
p1(x1 + Δx1) + p2(x2 + Δx2) = M ……(2.5)
Subtracting (2.4) from (2.5): p1Δx1 + p2Δx2 = 0 ……(2.6)
Rearranging: Δx2/Δx1 = − p1/p2 ……(2.7)

Meaning: the absolute slope p1/p2 is the rate at which the market lets the consumer substitute bananas for mangoes when she spends all her income.

Explain the Indifference Map with a diagram.4 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E25-2 (Q28), MQP24-3 (Q28)

Board answer key · E25-2

Family or group of Indifference curves is called Indifference Map. The arrow indicates that bundles on higher indifference curves are preferred by the consumer to the bundles on lower indifference curves.

Explanation · NCERT
  • An indifference curve joins all bundles of two goods that give the consumer the same satisfaction, so she is indifferent among them.
  • The consumer's preferences over all bundles can be shown by a family of indifference curves. This family is called the indifference map.
  • Preferences are monotonic (more of a good is preferred to less). So bundles on a curve that lies above are preferred to bundles on a curve that lies below: IC4 is preferred to IC3, IC3 to IC2, and IC2 to IC1.
  • Features seen in the map:
    • Each curve slopes downward from left to right: to get more bananas, the consumer must give up some mangoes.
    • Each curve is convex to the origin, because of the law of diminishing marginal rate of substitution (MRS).
    • A higher curve gives a higher level of utility.
    • Two indifference curves never intersect each other.

1-mark links: "Two indifference curves never ________ each other." Answer: Intersect (E26-2 key). Match item "Indifference Map": A family of indifference curves.

Micro Ch 1 · IntroductionUsually Q27

What are the three central problems of an economy? Explain.4 marksAsked 4× · 2 board exams

Asked in: E26-2, E25-3, MQP24-3, MQP-3 (all Q27)
Also asked as: "What are the central problems of an economy? Explain" (E25-3), "Briefly explain the central problems of an economy." (MQP24-3)
Also covers: "Mention the Central problems of an economy." (2 marks, MQP-4)

Board answer key · E26-2
  1. What is produced in what quantities? Or What to produce?
  2. How these goods are produced? Or How to produce?
  3. For whom these goods are produced? Or Whom to produce?
Explanation · NCERT

Production, exchange and consumption are the basic economic activities. Every society faces scarcity of resources, and scarcity gives rise to the problem of choice. Because scarce resources have competing uses, every society must decide how to use them:

  • What to produce and in what quantities? How much of each good and service to produce. For example: more food, clothing and housing, or more luxury goods; more agricultural goods or more industrial goods and services; more resources for education and health, or for the military; more consumption goods, or more investment goods (like machines) that raise production tomorrow.
  • How to produce? How much of which resources to use for each good. For example: more labour or more machines, and which of the available technologies to adopt.
  • For whom to produce? How the output is distributed among people: who gets more and who gets less. For example: whether to ensure a minimum amount of consumption for everyone, and whether elementary education and basic health services should be free for all.

One-line conclusion (NCERT): the allocation of scarce resources and the distribution of the final goods and services are the central problems of any economy.

Briefly explain the production possibility frontier.4 marksAsked 3× · 1 board exam

Board answer key · E25-2

Production possibility frontier (PPF) represents the production possibilities of two goods that can be produced by an economy with maximum/full utilization of its limited resources. Or PPF is the graphical representation of Production possibility set.

Explanation · NCERT
  • An economy's resources are limited. All the combinations of two goods that can be produced from a given amount of resources and a given technology form the production possibility set.
  • In the example, using all resources for corn gives at most 4 units of corn. Using all of them for cotton gives at most 10 units of cotton. B, C and D are other possible combinations.
  • The PPF is the curve showing the maximum amount of one good that can be produced for any given amount of the other, when resources are fully used.
  • A point strictly below the PPF means some resources are underemployed or used wastefully.
  • Using more resources for corn leaves fewer for cotton. The amount of the other good given up to get one more unit of a good is its opportunity cost.
  • The economy must choose one point among these possibilities. This choice is itself one of the central problems.

How does the Market economy work? Explain.4 marksAsked 2× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E26-1 (Q27), MQP-4 (Q27)

Board answer key · E26-1

A market economy is one in which all economic activities are conducted by economic agents in the market, such as buyers and sellers instead of the government. In a market, sellers and buyers can exchange goods in a village square or in a city supermarket, or through communication with each other over the telephone or the Internet. Thus, in a market system, price signals coordinate economic activity, resolving the central problems of what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce.

Explanation · NCERT
  • Meaning of market: a market is an institution that organises the free interaction of individuals. It is a set of arrangements where economic agents can freely exchange their endowments or products with each other.
  • Not a physical place: buyers and sellers need not meet. They can trade at a village chowk, a super bazaar, or over the telephone or internet.
  • Prices: every good or service has a price, agreed between buyers and sellers, at which exchange takes place. The price reflects, on average, society's valuation of that good.
  • Price signals: if buyers demand more of a good, its price rises. This tells producers that society wants more of it, so they produce more. In this way prices carry information to everyone in the market.
  • Coordination: through these price signals, the activities of millions of separate individuals are coordinated, and the central problems of what and how much to produce are solved.

Demand rises → price rises → producers produce more. In reality, all economies are mixed. The USA is close to a market economy, with a minimal role for the government.

Discuss how the Family Farm, Weaver and Teacher can use their resources to fulfill their needs in a simple economy.4 marksAsked 2× · model papers only

Asked in: MQP24-1 (Q27), MQP-2 (Q27)
Also asked as: "Briefly explain, how the Family Farm, Weaver, Teacher can use their resources to fulfil their needs in a simple economy." (MQP-2)

Answer · NCERT

People in a society need many goods and services, but no individual has all the things she needs. Each one has only a few resources. Each one uses her resources to produce something, and exchanges part of it for the other goods and services she needs.

UnitResources it hasHow it fulfils its needs
Family farmA plot of land, some grains, farming implements, maybe a pair of bullocks, and the labour of family membersProduces corn, uses part of it for its own consumption, and gets clothing, housing and services in exchange for the rest
WeaverYarn, cotton and other instruments for weavingProduces cloth and gets the goods and services she wants in exchange for it
TeacherThe skills needed to educate studentsEarns money by teaching in the school and uses it to buy the goods and services she wants
LabourerOnly her own labourEarns money by working for someone else and uses it to meet her needs
  • Scarcity and choice: no one has unlimited resources compared to their needs. So each unit must choose: it can have more of one thing only by giving up some of another. For example, a bigger house may mean giving up some farmland, and better education for the children may mean giving up some luxuries.
  • Society as a whole: what people collectively want must match what is produced. For example, total corn produced must match the corn people want. If not, resources should be shifted to other goods.
  • So a simple economy faces two basic problems: allocating its limited resources among different goods, and distributing the goods produced among its people.

List out the differences between Micro and Macroeconomics.4 marksAsked 4× · 2 board exams

Asked in: E26-2, E25-2, MQP24-3, MQP-2 (all Q31)

Board answer key · E25-2 (the E26-2 key gives the same points except row 6)
Micro economicsMacro economics
1. Studies individual units1. Studies aggregate units
2. Micro means small2. Macro means large
3. Deals with individual market demand and supply3. Deals with all market demand and supply
4. Partial equilibrium analysis4. General equilibrium analysis
5. Slicing method5. Lumping method
6. Worm's view6. Bird's view

Explanation · NCERT
  • Microeconomics studies the behaviour of individual economic agents (consumers and producers) in the markets for different goods. It finds how the price and quantity of each good are determined. It mostly studies particular sectors, assuming the rest of the economy stays the same. Inflation and unemployment are taken as given.
  • Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, using aggregate measures like total output, employment and the general price level. It asks: How is total output determined? How does it grow? Are resources fully employed? Why do prices rise? It also takes into account the interlinkages between sectors.
  • Decision makers: micro agents try to maximise their own profit or satisfaction. Macro decision makers (the State, and bodies like RBI and SEBI) pursue public goals for the welfare of the country as a whole.
Write about the role of the Government (State) and Household sectors in both developed and developing countries.4 marksAsked 3× · 1 board exam

Asked in: E26-1 (Q31), MQP24-2 (Q30), MQP-1 (Q31)
Also asked as: "Explain the role of the Government and household sector in both developed and developing nations." (MQP24-2)

Board answer key · E26-1

Role of Government (State):

  1. Framing and enforcing laws
  2. Delivering justice
  3. Imposing taxes and collection
  4. Building infrastructures – providing schools, colleges, health services etc.

Role of Households:

  1. A household is a person who makes decisions about their own well-being or a group of people who jointly make decisions about well-being.
  2. Savings and paying taxes
  3. Working and earning wages
  4. Making profits as owners of businesses, etc.
Explanation · NCERT
  • Government: in both developed and developing countries, there is the State alongside the private capitalist sector. Besides making laws, enforcing them and delivering justice, the State often undertakes production itself. It also imposes taxes and spends money on public infrastructure, schools, colleges and health services. These economic functions must be counted when describing a country's economy. For convenience, the State is called the "Government".
  • Households: a household is a single individual who takes decisions about her own consumption, or a group whose consumption decisions are made jointly. Households also save and pay taxes.
  • Where household income comes from: wages from working in firms, salaries from government jobs, profits as owners of firms, rent from leasing land, and interest from lending capital.
  • Why households matter: the market in which firms sell their products could not function without the demand coming from households.

Write about the emergence of Macroeconomics.4 marksAsked 2× · model papers only

Asked in: MQP-3 (Q31), MQP-4 (Q31)
Also covers: the 1-mark questions on "Classical thought", the year of the Great Depression, and Keynes's 1936 book

Answer · NCERT
  • Birth of the subject: macroeconomics emerged as a separate branch after the British economist John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money in 1936.
  • Classical tradition: before Keynes, economists believed that all labourers who are ready to work will find employment, and all factories will work at full capacity.
  • The Great Depression of 1929: in the years that followed, output and employment fell by huge amounts in Europe and North America, and other countries were affected too. Demand for goods was low, many factories lay idle, and workers lost their jobs.
  • Size of the fall in the USA: from 1929 to 1933, the unemployment rate rose from 3% to 25%, and aggregate output fell by about 33%.
  • Why a new subject was needed: these events made economists think about the economy in a new way. Long-lasting unemployment had to be explained. Keynes's book tried to do this.
  • Keynes's approach: unlike earlier economists, he examined the working of the economy as a whole and the interdependence of its different sectors. With this, the subject of macroeconomics was born.

Unemployment rate (NCERT) = number of people who are not working and are looking for jobs ÷ total number of people who are working or looking for jobs. Macroeconomics still has deep roots in microeconomics, because it studies the combined effects of demand and supply in markets.
